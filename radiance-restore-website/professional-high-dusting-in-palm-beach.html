Professional High Dusting in Palm Beach, FL

Serving Palm Beach, Port St. Lucie, and Fort Pierce

Home›Cleaning Guides›High Dusting

Cleaning above eye level: ceiling corners, vents, light fixtures, tops of cabinets, door frames, and fan blades. The areas that almost never get touched between deep cleans.

High dusting is work most people notice only once it has been done. The room reads brighter, the air stops carrying that faint fuzz, and surfaces stay clean longer because nothing is drifting down onto them a day later. It gets booked before entertaining, ahead of a season opening when a house has been closed for months, after a renovation, and by clients who have realized their regular clean stops at about six feet. In an older Palm Beach house with tall ceilings, that leaves a lot of untouched square footage. Cabinet tops, beam ledges, transom sills, the flat upper edge of every door casing, and the ceiling corners where cobwebs anchor and stay.

What's Included

  • Ceiling corners and wall-to-ceiling joints cleared of cobwebs with an extension duster
  • Fan blades wiped top and bottom by hand, so dust lifts off instead of dropping to the floor
  • Air vent louvers dusted, then wiped where the grime has gone greasy near the kitchen
  • Tops of cabinets, armoires, bookcases, door frames, and window casings wiped down
  • Light fixtures and glass shades dusted and wiped, with bulbs and wiring left alone
  • Crown molding, beam ledges, and the top edges of picture frames detailed along the ledge
  • Transom windows, plate rails, and the upper sash of tall windows dusted where reachable
  • Smoke detector housings and speaker grilles dusted lightly without pressing on the units

Poles, Ladders, and the Order the Work Has to Happen In

The kit is simple. An extension pole with a microfiber sleeve for ceilings, corners, and long ledges. A step ladder for anything that needs a hand on it, which is most fixtures and every fan blade. A dry cloth for the first lift, and a barely damp one for anything that has gone tacky. Near a kitchen that means the vent louvers and the tops of the upper cabinets, where airborne cooking grease binds the dust into a film a duster only pushes around.

Sequence is the other half of it. High dusting happens first, before any surface is wiped and before floors are vacuumed or mopped, so what comes loose is picked up by the passes that follow. Fan blades get wiped rather than swept, one hand under the blade and a cloth over the top, so the load ends up in the cloth, not on the bed below. A cheaper visit skips the ladder entirely, runs a pole over the fan from the ground, and calls the ceiling done.

Why This Matters in Palm Beach

The older estate homes and Mediterranean-revival houses around Palm Beach were built with height. Twelve-foot ceilings, exposed beams and coffered panels, transom windows above the doors, cabinet tops nobody has looked at since the kitchen went in. That height is exactly why the dust up there never gets handled during a normal clean, and why it keeps drifting back down onto surfaces that were wiped an hour earlier. Ceiling fans running most of the year make it worse, pushing a steady haze out toward the wall corners and packing a felt of dust onto the leading edge of every blade. Add the plaster and stucco textures common in these houses, which hold dust in a way smooth drywall does not, then a formal room with a chandelier and heavy crown molding, and you have a great deal of ledge at a height nobody reaches. So high dusting is a poles-and-step-ladder job, and it goes first in the sequence, so anything that falls is caught by the surface and floor work that follows behind it.

What Gets Missed When High Dusting Is Rushed

The giveaways are consistent. The top of the refrigerator and the cabinet run above it. The back half of each fan blade, which looks fine from below and holds a strip of gray felt on the upper face. Door casings, which are flat, horizontal, and at exactly the height nobody checks. Vent louvers in a hallway, where the fins get dusted on the visible face and left loaded on the inside edge where the air actually moves.

There are limits worth stating plainly. We work from extension poles and step ladders at normal interior ceiling heights. We do not use tall ladders, scaffolding, or lifts, we do not disassemble or lower fixtures, and we do not enter attic spaces or clean inside ductwork. Two-storey entry ceilings and open stairwell voids are usually beyond safe reach, and when that is the case we say so at the walkthrough rather than leaving it half done.

Frequently Asked Questions

How high can you actually reach? Extension poles and step ladders cover normal interior ceiling heights, which includes most fans, flush fixtures, and cabinet tops. We do not work from tall ladders, scaffolding, or lifts, and we do not take down heavy chandeliers or remove fixtures to clean them. Open stairwell ceilings are usually beyond safe reach.
Will the dust just land on everything below? That is why sequence matters. High dusting is done before the surfaces are wiped and before floors are vacuumed and mopped, so whatever comes loose gets picked up by the passes that follow. Fan blades are wiped by hand rather than swept, and bedding under a fan gets covered first or vacuumed afterward.
Is high dusting part of a standard clean? Ceiling fans, light fixtures, air vents, light switches, and outlet covers are dusted and wiped as part of Deep Cleaning. Standard Cleaning covers door frames, shelves, woodwork, and window sills at normal reach. High dusting can also be booked on its own, or added to a recurring visit.
Do you clean chandeliers and pendant lights? We dust and wipe fixtures and glass shades in place, which handles most of what dulls them. We do not remove crystal drops, take fixtures down, disconnect wiring, or change bulbs. A large multi-tier chandelier over a stairwell needs a specialist with proper access equipment, and we will say so.
How often should high dusting be booked? Two to four times a year suits most homes, and more often where ceiling fans run continuously or a house sits closed between seasons. If cobwebs are back in the corners within a few weeks, that is usually airflow from a vent or a lanai door left open, and a shorter interval makes more sense than a heavier product.
