What estate cleaning actually involves

In a house with a guest wing, two kitchens and a garage that holds four cars, cleaning stops being a visit and becomes a route. Rooms get grouped, a team works in an agreed order, and somebody keeps track of what was done when. Guest suites nobody has slept in still need attention, because dust settles the same in an empty room and a Florida summer will find any surface left alone for a month.

The standard is different too. In an ordinary house, clean means clean. In a large home it means nothing looks as though anybody was there: cushions and throws back exactly as they were, nothing rearranged on a dressing table, no cleaning smell in the air an hour later. We agree the finish at the walkthrough, room by room where it matters, and we write it down so the answer does not depend on which cleaner is standing in it.

  • Room-by-room scope agreed in writing
  • A consistent team rather than whoever is free
  • Guest suites and unused rooms on a rotation
  • Detail work scheduled rather than squeezed in
  • An agreed do-not-touch list for every home
  • Set arrival and finish times you can plan around

Working alongside a house manager and the rest of the household

Most estates we clean already have people coming and going: a house or estate manager, a chef, a nanny, pool and landscape crews, and whoever is dealing with the AV system that week. The job is to fit into that without adding friction. We take direction from whoever runs the house, we work to the household calendar, and we keep to whichever days and hours suit everyone else's schedule rather than ours.

Practical things matter more than they sound. Who opens up, and who arms the alarm at the end. Which doors stay closed. Whether the dogs are in or out. Where our vehicles park so a delivery is not blocked. Which rooms are off-limits when a guest is staying. Agree it once, write it down, and a new person on the team learns it from the file rather than by getting it wrong.

Discretion is part of the work, not an add-on

Cleaning a private home means seeing it, and the people who see it should be people you have some basis to trust. Every one of our cleaners is background-checked, reference-checked, interviewed in person, insured and trained. That is the same standard we hold everywhere, and it matters most in a house where the cleaners are inside far more often than the owners are.

The rest is behaviour. We do not photograph a client's home for our website or social media unless we are asked and given permission in writing. We do not discuss whose house we clean, what is in it, or who was there. We are happy to sign your confidentiality agreement, and if your household has its own rules on phones, deliveries or vendor conduct, they go into our file for the property before the first visit.

The closed season: a big house on its own from May to November

Seasonal ownership is normal here, and an empty estate is not an idle one. Air conditioning left running all summer keeps humidity in check but works its condensate lines and ducts hard, and the EPA is direct that controlling moisture is the most effective way to prevent biological growth in ductwork. Meanwhile dust settles undisturbed, and a house shut for six months develops a smell that residents notice within thirty seconds of walking in.

So most seasonal clients book two things. Maintenance visits while the house is empty, monthly or on whatever rhythm suits the property, which keeps dust down and puts eyes on rooms nobody else enters. Then an opening clean timed to arrival, ideally a few days before the flight rather than the morning of it. Beds made with fresh linen, glass done, the kitchen ready, and storm panels dealt with before anyone arrives to find them still up.

  • Monthly maintenance visits while the house is closed
  • Opening cleans scheduled to your arrival date
  • Closing cleans before the house is shut for summer
  • Guest suites prepared ahead of visitors
  • Post-storm cleaning once shutters come down
  • Extra visits around events and family gatherings

Marble, wood, art and the chandelier nobody can reach

Large homes are full of surfaces where the wrong product is an expensive mistake. Marble and other natural stone, unlacquered brass, specialty wood finishes, silk, hand-knotted rugs. Our rule is simple: if we are not certain, we ask before we touch it, and where a surface has a maintenance contractor already, we leave it to them. That is not caution for its own sake. It is cheaper for everyone than a conversation about a countertop.

Fine art, antiques and anything of genuine collection value sit outside general cleaning entirely, and reputable conservation guidance such as the National Park Service's Conserve O Gram leaflets exists precisely because these objects need specialists. Same with chandeliers that require a lift, and anything requiring work at height. We set out what falls outside our scope at the walkthrough, because a shorter list you can rely on is worth more.

How estate cleaning is priced and scheduled

Pricing for estate cleaning in Palm Beach Gardens FL starts with a walkthrough, always. From that we quote a flat figure with the scope attached, so you know what the number buys. We are reachable 24 hours a day, which matters when guests arrive early or a plan changes at nine at night. Our guarantee holds here as it does everywhere: if something is not right, tell us within 24 hours and we return and put it right at no charge.

Frequently Asked Questions

How is estate cleaning different from regular house cleaning? Scale changes the method. A large home needs a team working a planned route, a written room-by-room scope, and a rotation so unused rooms are not neglected. It also involves coordination with household staff and a higher bar on discretion and finish. The underlying work is the same, but the planning around it is not.
Can you work with our house manager or estate manager? Yes, and it usually makes everything smoother. We take direction from whoever runs the household, work to their calendar, and report back the way they prefer. If your manager wants a checklist signed each visit or a note left after we finish, we will do that. It is their house to run, not ours.
Will you sign a confidentiality agreement? Yes. Send it over before we start and we will sign it. We do not discuss clients, homes or who is staying in them, and we never photograph a home for our website or social media without written permission. Our cleaners are background-checked, reference-checked and interviewed in person before they set foot in any house.
Can you keep an eye on the house while we are away for the summer? We can clean on a set schedule while a home is empty, and our teams will tell you if they see something obviously wrong, like water where water should not be. We are not a licensed property watch or security service, though, and we will not present ourselves as one. Many clients use both.
Do you clean art, antiques or high chandeliers? No. Fine art, antiques and collection pieces need conservation specialists, and anything requiring a lift or work at height is outside what we do. We clean around them carefully and we ask before touching anything we are unsure of. A short, reliable list beats a long, optimistic one.
Can you handle linens and bed changes? Simple household linen work can sit in the scope, such as changing beds and making up guest rooms before arrivals. Agree it at the walkthrough so it is priced properly and nobody is guessing. Specialist laundry, delicate fabrics and dry-clean items are better left with whoever already handles them for you.
How far ahead should we book an opening clean before season? As soon as you know your arrival date. October and November fill up quickly here, because half the county is opening houses in the same few weeks. Booking early also lets us schedule a couple of days before you land, so you walk into a finished house rather than past a vacuum.
